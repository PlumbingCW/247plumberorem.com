What fails inside the tank and under the bowl.

A toilet has two jobs: refill the tank to a set level, then release that water through the bowl in one fast surge. The refill side depends on the fill valve and float. The release side depends on the flapper, the chain, the flush lever and the rim jets under the bowl lip.

A running toilet is almost always a flapper that no longer seals, or a chain that is too tight or too slack. A phantom refill every few minutes means water is slipping past the flapper into the bowl. A hissing tank usually points to a fill valve that cannot shut off cleanly.

The base is a different system. A wax ring or foam gasket seals the closet flange on the floor to the horn of the bowl. When the bowl rocks even slightly, that seal breaks, and water seeps out only during a flush, so the damage hides under vinyl or tile for weeks.

Overflowing toilet: what to do first.

Stop the inflow before anything else. Reach behind the toilet and close the small oval supply stop clockwise. If it will not turn, remove the tank lid and lift the float arm or press the flapper into its seat to cut the refill.

Do not flush again, and do not plunge a bowl that is already full to the rim. If water has reached outlets, baseboards or a wall below, keep people away from wet electrical areas and shut power off at the panel only if the panel is dry and you can reach it safely.

Call right away if the toilet backs up when you run a sink or shower, because that suggests a blocked drain line, not a bowl problem. Late-night overflows and weekend failures get the same 24/7 dispatch, with same-day appointments when available for calmer jobs like a slow leak at the base.

How a toilet repair is carried out.

The plumber begins with the water: supply stop, braided connector, tank bolts and tank-to-bowl gasket are all checked for drips with dry tissue. Then the tank is opened, the flapper and fill valve are tested, and dye tablets show whether water is leaking into the bowl.

Fixing the base means draining the tank, disconnecting the supply, unbolting the bowl and lifting it clear. The old wax is scraped off the flange, the flange is checked for cracks or a loose mount, and the bowl is reset on new wax or a gasket with fresh closet bolts. A flange sitting below the finished floor may need a spacer ring.

A bowl that backs up or gurgles is a drain problem, so the work moves to the line. That is where clearing the drain line with a cable or auger comes in, since no amount of tank work will help a blocked branch.

Parts versus fixture

A flush kit or fill valve is a small parts job. A cracked bowl or tank means a new fixture, and the model you choose changes the total.

Condition of the flange

A broken or low flange adds repair parts and time. A sound flange keeps the visit short.

Supply stop and connector age

A seized or leaking stop valve is replaced before the toilet is reconnected. That adds a part and a shutoff to the visit.

Drain blockage

If the problem is in the line, cabling or augering becomes the main task, and the time depends on how deep the blockage sits.

Gather this before you call about a toilet.

A few minutes of observation saves diagnostic time and helps the plumber bring the right parts.

  • Note whether the leak shows during a flush, between flushes or only when the tank refills.
  • Press on the bowl gently from the side to see if it rocks, and note whether the floor feels soft nearby.
  • Look for the make and age on the tank lid or inside the tank, which helps match a flush kit.
  • Find the manufacture stamp inside the tank if you plan a replacement, and measure the distance from the wall to the closet bolts (the rough-in).
  • Check whether other drains in the house are slow, since that points to the line and not the fixture.
  • Take photos of any water staining on the ceiling below, then shut the supply stop and leave it closed.
