Repair or replace: how the decision usually falls.

Most replacement calls come down to the same few facts. This table shows how a plumber weighs them.

Age alone does not condemn a heater. A well-kept tank can outlast an average one, and a plumber's judgment on a dry, clean tank may be to keep it running.

Why tanks fail and what it means for the new one.

A glass-lined steel tank fails from the inside. Hot water and dissolved minerals attack any bare steel, and a sacrificial anode rod is there to take the corrosion first. When the rod is gone, pits form in the lining and eventually go through the shell. That is why a leak from the tank body cannot be repaired.

The cause matters when choosing the next unit. A household that outgrew a 40 gallon tank may need 50 gallons or more. A basement that floods may call for a unit raised on a platform. A family tired of running out may consider a hybrid heat pump unit or the option on the tankless water heater page.

Fuel changes, such as gas to a heat pump unit, add electrical and venting work, so ask for that to be priced separately from a like-for-like swap. Sizing should come from your household, not a rule of thumb. The first-hour rating on the unit's yellow label matters more than gallon count, because it shows how much hot water the heater can deliver in a busy morning.

What happens on a replacement visit.

The sequence is the same for most tanks, even when the details change.

  1. Confirm the failure

    The plumber checks where the water is coming from and whether the tank, a fitting or a nearby pipe is the real source.

  2. Shut down and drain

    Water, fuel and power are isolated, then the tank is drained to a floor drain or through a hose to the outside.

  3. Remove the old unit

    Connectors, vent pipe and gas or electric lines come off, and the old tank is carried out.

  4. Set and connect the new heater

    New flex lines, a relief valve and discharge pipe, and an expansion tank where the system needs one, go in. Gas units get a leak test on the joints.

  5. Test and walk through

    The unit is fired up, the vent and temperature are checked, and the plumber shows you the shutoff valve and the thermostat.

Before the plumber arrives.

A few minutes of preparation saves time on the visit.

  • Photograph the water and the damage before cleanup, for your insurance claim.
  • Clear a path to the heater and a few feet around it, including stored boxes.
  • Read the label on the old tank and note the capacity, fuel type and model number.
  • Find the main water shutoff and make sure it turns, because stuck valves are common in older homes.
  • Keep pets and children out of the utility room until the floor is dry.
  • Decide whether you want the same type of heater back or want to hear about a heat pump or tankless option.

What shapes the cost of a replacement.

Two houses with the same tank can get very different totals. These are the reasons.

Unit type and capacity

A standard gas tank, a 50 gallon electric tank, a hybrid heat pump unit and a tankless heater sit at very different levels.

Fuel changes and new venting

Switching fuel can mean a new gas line, a new circuit or a new vent path. Like-for-like swaps are simpler.

Code items

Expansion tanks, platform support and a proper discharge line may be required, and permits may add a fee.

Access and disposal

Narrow stairs, an attic location or a crawl space adds time. Hauling the old tank away is part of the job.

After-hours timing

A night or weekend emergency call is billed differently than a planned weekday install.

A tank that bursts and floods a room is usually a sudden and accidental loss that homeowners policies typically cover. Replacement of a heater worn out by age typically is not, and gradual leaks and groundwater flooding are generally excluded. Read your own policy and ask your insurer what documentation it wants.
