A sump pump sits in a pit and moves water out of the house before it reaches the floor. Utah Geological Survey guidance calls for an annual winter check of every sump pump, timed ahead of the groundwater season. Water coming up in the basement right now: what to do first.

First, check the electrical situation. Do not step into standing water if the pump, power strips or the panel are in it. Switch power off at the breaker panel only when the floor around it is dry and you can get there without wading. Otherwise stay out and call.

If the pit is full and the pump is silent, look at the plug and the breaker. A tripped circuit or a loose plug is the most common reason a pump stops, and it takes seconds to check from a dry spot. Do not pull a plug while standing in water.

Lift boxes, rugs and electronics off the floor, and run a bucket brigade only if the water is shallow and clear of electrical hazards. Describe what you see when you call: pit full or empty, pump humming or silent. Dispatch covers nights and weekends, and same-day appointments are offered when available.

Why sump pumps fail.

Most pumps fail in one of a few ways. The float switch snags on the pit wall or wedges in debris, so the pump never starts or never shuts off. A pump that runs constantly may be burning out its motor. A seized impeller hums but moves no water.

A failed check valve lets water fall back into the pit after each cycle, so the pump short-cycles and wears out. A frozen or clogged discharge line has nowhere to push water, and the pit fills even though the pump runs. Power loss during a storm is the other classic: the pump is fine, but nothing powers it.

Primary pumps take the load most of the time, and a backup pump or a battery-powered unit takes over when the primary fails or the power drops. Homes with no backup have no margin for a bad night. Ejector pumps are a related device that lifts sewage and wastewater from a basement bathroom up to the sewer line. They are built for solids and are not interchangeable with a sump pump.

Not every wet basement is a pump problem. Seepage along the joint where the floor meets the wall may be groundwater pressure, which is a basement waterproofing question, and a floor drain that gurgles up is usually a drain clog. A damp spot that appears in dry weather with a quiet pit can also be a supply line, and leak detection finds that.

What happens during a sump pump visit.

The goal is to decide fast whether the pump needs a repair or a replacement.

  1. Check power and the pit

    The technician checks the outlet, the breaker and the pit for debris, then pours water into the pit to see whether the float lifts and the pump starts.

  2. Test the pump and valve

    The pump runs through a full cycle. The check valve is inspected for backflow, and the discharge line is traced to find kinks, ice or blockage.

  3. Decide repair or replace

    A stuck float or a clogged intake is a small repair. A burned motor or a cracked housing usually means a new pump.

  4. Add protection if needed

    A battery backup, a second pump or a high-water alarm can be added so a power failure does not leave the pit unprotected.

  5. Run a final test

    The pit is filled again to confirm the pump starts, empties it and shuts off without short-cycling.

Repair or replace: how the options compare.

Pump choices depend on how old the unit is, how it failed and how much water the pit handles.

Replace the float switchThe pump motor is healthy but the switch is stuck or wornInexpensive and quick, but only helps if the motor is sound.

A pump that is more than a decade old and has begun to cycle strangely is usually a replacement, not a repair. A plumber can tell you from the model and the symptoms.

Before you call about a sump pump.

A few answers shorten the diagnosis.

  • Say whether the pit is full, half full or empty and whether the water level is rising.
  • Note whether the pump is silent, humming, running nonstop or cycling on and off.
  • Check that the pump is plugged in and that the circuit breaker and any GFCI outlet have not tripped, only from a dry spot.
  • Look at the discharge pipe outside and note ice, a crushed section or an outlet that drains near the foundation.
  • Write down the pump brand and any label details so the technician can match a part.
  • Tell the dispatcher if you have a battery backup or a second pump and whether the alarm sounded.
  • Photograph any wet areas and the damage for insurance before you move belongings.

What affects sump pump repair and replacement cost.

A stuck float and a full pump replacement with a backup system are different jobs, and the quote follows what the technician finds.

Failed component

A float or check valve is a small part. A motor failure usually means a new pump.

Pump size and type

Horsepower, flow rate and head height must match the pit and the discharge route, and ejector pumps differ from sump pumps.

Backup systems

Battery backups, second pumps and alarms add equipment and installation work.

Discharge line condition

Clearing, replacing or rerouting the line changes the scope.

Timing and conditions

Active flooding after hours calls for faster scheduling than a planned winter inspection.
