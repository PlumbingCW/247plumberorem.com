Provo plumbing starts with the year the house was built.

Provo was founded in 1849 as Fort Utah. Census counts show how long it has been building: 6,185 residents in 1900, 14,766 in 1930, 28,937 in 1950 and 36,047 in 1960. Those older neighborhoods predate Orem's postwar boom by decades, so a Provo house is more likely than an Orem one to have a century of repairs layered into its pipes.

The payoff is practical. A home from the early 1900s may still have original steel supply lines or old clay and cast iron drains under the yard, while a 1950s house often has a mix of copper and cast iron with later patches. Tell the dispatcher the build decade and whether any repipe was done, and the plumber arrives with the right parts instead of guessing.

Emergency in Provo: what to do first.

Water you cannot stop is the first priority. Close the main shutoff, usually where the line enters the house near the water heater or a basement wall, by turning it clockwise. In an older home that valve may be stiff, so turn it slowly and never force it with a pipe wrench.

For a sewage backup, stop running water and flushing. If a gas smell is present, leave the building and call the gas utility or 911 before anything else. Emergency calls from Provo are answered 24/7, and same-day appointments are offered when available.

Water heaters in Provo: the permit step most people miss.

In Provo, a water heater swap is permitted work. The city's Building Division lists water heaters alongside furnaces and air conditioning as jobs that need a building permit, with an inspection requested once the job is complete. Permits are filed online, and the division's email is buildingpermits@provo.utah.gov.

That matters on a cold night when a tank fails. A plumber who handles water heater replacement should pull the permit and plan for the inspection, so ask on the phone who is responsible for it. If the tank is leaking from the base, shut off the cold supply and the gas or power first, then call.

Near Utah Lake and the Provo River: water in the ground.

Provo sits on the eastern bank of Utah Lake, and the Provo River runs through it. Utah Geological Survey guidance says shallow groundwater, generally within 10 feet of the surface, is more likely in low valley areas and near rivers, streams and lakes. It can flood basements and damage buried lines.

For a Provo homeowner, an unexplained wet basement may be groundwater rather than a pipe, and the two need different fixes. A technician using leak detection methods such as acoustic listening and a pressure test can rule a supply leak in or out. Standard homeowners insurance typically does not cover groundwater flooding, so check your own policy.

Before we arrive in Provo: four things to do.

These steps take a few minutes and make the first visit shorter, whatever the house's age.

Find the main shutoff

Locate it before you need it and test that it turns. In an older house it may sit in a crawl space, a cellar corner or a meter pit.

Note the build decade

Tell the dispatcher when the house was built and what has been replaced. That one detail narrows the likely pipe material.

Photograph the damage

Take pictures of the leak, the water line on the wall and the water heater label. They help any insurance claim and the plumber's plan.

Clear the work area

Move boxes and furniture away from the heater, the sink base or the cleanout. Keep children and pets out of wet rooms.
