How to Use a Plumbing Snake on a Kitchen Sink: A Guide for Orem, UT Homes

A snake can clear a kitchen clog in minutes, or it can make a mess. Start with the trap and know when to stop.

By the Orem 24/7 Plumbing team · Published October 23, 2026

Call (385) 488-1892See all services

A kitchen sink drains grease, food scraps and soap into a branch line that is narrower than the main drain, so it clogs more often than other fixtures in the house. A hand-cranked drain snake, also called an auger, is the standard tool for clearing it. Used well, it breaks up or hooks the blockage. Used badly, it scratches the trap, jams in a bend, or pushes the clog deeper.

This guide explains the order of operations, how to feed the cable, what a snake cannot fix, and when high-pressure water or a professional is the better route.

Check the easy causes before you reach for the snake.

Start by finding out whether the blockage is in the sink or farther down the line. Run the cold water and watch whether other fixtures, such as a dishwasher or a bathroom sink, back up or gurgle. If only the kitchen sink is slow, the problem is probably in the trap or the short branch line behind it.

A dishwasher drain hose connects to the disposal or the sink tailpiece, so a stuck dishwasher can be the same clog in disguise. If you have a garbage disposal, make sure it is not jammed and the switch is off before you put hands or tools near it.

If you have a disposal and the trap is clear, the clog may sit on the disposal side of the sink, in the dishwasher drain connection, or in the trap arm just past the trap. A dishwasher that fills the sink when it drains is a sign the blockage is downstream of both.

Step by step: clearing a kitchen sink with a hand auger.

Put a bucket and towels under the trap and wear gloves. Do not mix drain chemicals with any of this.

  1. Open the trap

    Place the bucket under the U-shaped trap, loosen the slip nuts by hand or with pliers, and pull the trap. Empty it and clean out anything stuck in it. Many kitchen clogs end here.

  2. Feed the cable

    If the trap is clear, push the end of the auger into the wall stub-out and turn the handle clockwise while you feed cable in. Let the tip do the work rather than forcing it through a bend.

  3. Work the blockage

    When you feel resistance, keep turning and pull back a little so the tip can hook or break the clog. Retrieve the cable slowly and wipe it as it comes out.

  4. Reassemble and flush

    Reinstall the trap with the slip nuts snug, run hot water for a minute, and check for leaks at both joints.

Why kitchen clogs come back.

Grease is the main culprit. It leaves the sink liquid, cools in the pipe, and builds a coat on the pipe wall that narrows the opening over months. A snake bores a hole through that coat but leaves most of it in place, which is why a kitchen line can clog again within weeks.

Running hot water and dish soap after greasy dishes helps a little, but it does not remove a mature layer. When a clog returns repeatedly, hydro jetting cleans the pipe wall with high-pressure water instead of just poking a channel through it. A technician can tell you whether the line is a good candidate before you commit.

When to stop and call a plumber.

Stop if the cable will not advance past a hard obstruction, if you hear a grinding sound, or if more than one fixture backs up. Stop if water comes up from a floor drain or a basement fixture, since that points to the main line rather than the sink.

An overnight clog that spills into the cabinet or floor may need an after-hours visit. A plumber with a camera can see what is going on in the line, and a drum machine cleans farther than a hand auger can reach. Our drain cleaning team handles kitchen branch lines and can say whether cabling or jetting suits the line. Ask which method the technician will bring when you call.

Before you buy or rent a snake.

A small kit makes the job easier and protects the pipe.

  • A hand auger of about 25 feet is plenty for a kitchen branch line.
  • Rubber gloves, safety glasses, a bucket and old towels.
  • A flashlight and a pair of slip-joint pliers for the trap nuts.
  • Replacement slip-joint washers in case the old ones are brittle.
  • A plan for the disposal: switch off, and never reach in by hand.
