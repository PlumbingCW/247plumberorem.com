Pipes fail in different ways. Some split along their length after a freeze, some crack at an elbow or joint, and some corrode through at a pinhole that grows. This page covers the causes, what a repair visit looks like, how repair choices compare, and what to have ready when you call.

Burst pipe emergency: what to do in the first few minutes.

Water volume is the enemy, so cut it at the source. The main shutoff is usually where the line enters the house, often near the water heater or in a utility room. If it will not turn or leaks around the stem, say so when you call, because that changes the plan.

Next, drain the system. Turn off the water heater first, at the gas control or the breaker, so an electric element does not run dry as the tank drains. Then open the faucets at the lowest point and flush one toilet. Empty pipes stop dripping, and that stops the damage from growing while you wait.

Stay out from under a ceiling that is bulging or sagging, since trapped water is heavy. Keep everyone away from wet outlets and the panel, and photograph the damage before you move things. Calls for burst lines get after-hours dispatch at night and on weekends, and same-day appointments are offered when available.

Why pipes crack, split and burst.

Freezing is the best-known cause. Water expands as it turns to ice, and the pressure builds between the ice plug and the closed faucet. The pipe often fails in the middle of a run, though the leak only shows when the ice thaws.

Copper tends to split lengthwise. PEX is more forgiving because it stretches, so the weak point becomes the fittings. PVC grows brittle in cold and can crack, which matters for exterior runs and drain lines in unheated spaces.

Other causes show up in homes of every age. Pressure spikes and water hammer fatigue joints. Corrosion eats thin spots in older steel and copper. A nail or screw driven into a wall during a remodel can pierce a line that holds for months. Settling soil stresses buried runs, and a break between the meter and the house becomes a water service line repair rather than an interior fix.

Not every burst is a pipe. A broken shutoff valve under a sink or behind a toilet, or a cracked washing machine valve, floods just as fast. Those are small parts that a plumber replaces after isolating the line. Drain pipes crack too, usually from settling or impact, and they leak only when the fixture drains.

What happens during a burst pipe visit.

Every job is different, but the sequence is steady.

  1. Secure the water

    The technician confirms the main is closed, isolates the broken zone with local valves where possible, and restores water to the rest of the house.

  2. Find the break

    A visible split is simple. A hidden one is traced with moisture readings and a pressure test, and leak detection takes over when the pipe is in a wall or under a slab.

  3. Cut out the damage

    The failed section is removed back to sound pipe, since a crack often runs farther than it looks.

  4. Rejoin the line

    Copper is joined with soldered or press fittings, PEX with crimp or expansion fittings, PVC with solvent cement. Material and joint type follow what is already in the wall.

  5. Test and document

    The line is pressurized and checked for weeping joints before the wall is closed. Keep the invoice and photos for your insurer.

Utah County cold snaps, the 30 inch frost line and frozen pipes.

Orem's design criteria list a frost line depth of 30 inches. Buried water lines are meant to sit below it, but the lines inside a house are not. Exterior wall runs, hose bibs, garage supply lines and crawlspace pipes sit above the frost line and can freeze in a hard cold snap.

If a pipe is frozen but not yet split, open the nearest faucet and warm the area slowly with a hair dryer or space heater placed safely away from the pipe. Never use an open flame. If you see a crack or a wet spot as the ice melts, close the main and call, because thawing often reveals the break.

Orem's housing boomed from 1950 to 1980, so many homes have supply lines that are now decades old. If a line has frozen and split more than once, ask the plumber whether the run should be replaced instead of patched.

Before you call about a cracked or broken pipe.

These details let dispatch plan the visit before anyone arrives.

  • Say where the water is coming from and whether it is running or just dripping.
  • Report whether the main shutoff closed fully and whether water still flows after it is closed.
  • Note the pipe material if you can see it: copper is orange-brown, PEX is flexible and usually red, blue or white, PVC is rigid white or gray.
  • Take photos of the broken pipe and the wet area before you clean up.
  • Clear a path to the shutoff and to the damaged area, including the space under a sink or around the water heater.
  • Tell the dispatcher about any wet ceilings, light fixtures or outlets near the water.
  • Pull out your homeowners policy number for the claim.

Copper, PEX and PVC use different fittings and tools, and larger diameters take more material and labor.

Access to the break

An exposed crawlspace line is quick. A line behind tile or inside a finished ceiling requires opening and closing the surface.

Extent of damage

One clean split is a small repair. A long crack or a run that has frozen in several places may need replacement of the full section.

Number of repairs

A single break and a whole zone of splits are different jobs even when the first call sounds similar.
